Transfer Target Optimisation Model

TypeSelf-directed project
Focus AreasFootball Finance, Applied Optimisation, Recruitment Analysis
ToolsExcel, AI-assisted development (Claude)
Key SkillsOptimisation Modelling, Financial Analysis, Directing AI Tools, Problem Framing

Deciding who to sign in a transfer window usually comes down to a scouting judgement call. I wanted to reframe it as an optimisation problem instead: given financial constraints and a pool of scouted targets, which combination of signings gets the most value without breaking the budget?

I built this because I'm genuinely interested in the finance side of football. I don't code myself, but I've learned to direct AI tools like Claude to turn my own logic and assumptions into a working model on Excel.

The model scores targets on utility, weighs them against cost and positional need, and solves for the best combination given the budget. It's the same logic that sits behind real transfer committee decisions, just made explicit and testable instead of left to gut feel.

Football's transfer market still runs largely on scouting relationships and valuation by feel, with financial discipline applied loosely at best. This is a small attempt at bringing some of that decision-making into a structured, repeatable framework, the sort of thing that could genuinely sit inside a recruitment or finance department rather than staying an academic exercise.

NFL UK Event Simulation

This was a group project where our team of six built a proposal for bringing an NFL game to the UK beyond London. We narrowed the host options down to two cities, Manchester's Etihad Stadium and Cardiff's Principality Stadium, and ultimately recommended Cardiff. My workstream was the data analysis, working out which fixture would pull the biggest UK audience so the matchup was chosen on evidence rather than gut feel.

I built a regression model to forecast UK viewership from factors like the teams involved, their standard and the timing of the game. A few findings shaped the proposal. Matchups between teams of a similar standard from the same division tended to draw better, so for each candidate host team, the Jaguars for Cardiff and the Dolphins for Manchester, I used the model to steer us towards sensible divisional opponents. It also showed that games between less glamorous teams had historically performed just as well over here, which told us the specific names mattered less than we had assumed and freed us up to weight other factors more heavily. The clearest result of all was timing: Sunday fixtures consistently brought in more viewers.

The analysis gave the rest of the proposal a solid base to build on, feeding into the spectator-market, branding, stadium-feasibility and grassroots sections the rest of the team developed, and we presented the whole thing to an academic panel. It was the project where I first saw how much sharper a commercial argument becomes when there is real data sitting underneath it.

TypeMSc Sports Business & Management dissertation

MethodSystematic literature review and case study

For my MSc dissertation I dug into a question I kept circling back to throughout the degree: what actually happens to a dominant football club, commercially, when it loses its only real rival? I approached it as a systematic literature review built around Celtic FC across the four seasons Rangers spent outside the Scottish top flight after their 2012 financial collapse and demotion. It gave me a rare natural experiment, the same dominant club in the same league, but with and without the one team that had ever been able to push them.

I grounded the work in the competitive balance theory Neale set out in 1964, then built out from it to look at how uncertainty of outcome and fan disengagement feed through into a club's commercial performance. Alongside the academic literature I brought in secondary evidence from the period, including Celtic's financial statements and reporting from the time, to test whether the story the research told actually matched what happened on the ground during Rangers' absence.

The review pulled together research from across European football, using other structurally dominant clubs like Bayern Munich, PSG and Dinamo Zagreb to frame the wider picture, before narrowing in on the uniquely lopsided case of Scottish football and the Old Firm. It came to around 7,500 words, and it is the piece of work that convinced me the commercial and financial side of football is where I want to build my career. A lot of what I take on now grew directly out of it.

MSc Sports Business & Management · University of Liverpool · 2025

How does the absence of competitive rivalry affect the wider commercial performance of a structurally dominant football club?

A case study of Celtic FC during Rangers FC’s relegation from the Scottish Premier League.

Introduction

A structurally dominant football club is the dominant force in an uncompetitive league. They possess a sustained and systematic competitive advantage which has lasted over many years, usually gained from historical success or through a large financial takeover. The club benefits from structural factors such as financial superiority, greater brand equity and a large fan base allowing the club to not only dominate on-field, but also in the club’s integration into culture, media, economics and even governance. A common occurrence in European Football is that a league is typically dominated by a select few clubs that challenge each other year on year, these clubs would be definable as structurally dominant clubs, but the existence of competition between multiple structurally dominant clubs naturally brings season-wide uncertainty of outcome which softens the impact of the lack of meaningful competition.

There are many examples of domestic leagues that have a structurally dominant club, such as, Croatia, where Dinamo Zagreb have won 25 of the 30 seasons ever played in the Croatian Football League or Germany, where Bayern Munich have won 12 of the past 13 seasons of the Bundesliga. With the recent financial expansion of European football, certain clubs have received huge fiscal backing, fast-tracking their success and becoming structurally dominant within a few years of investment. For example, Paris Saint-Germain in France, having received financial backing, established themselves as structurally dominant in their nation without major historical success. However, no nation is as unbalanced as Scotland, where in the top flight’s entire existence, Celtic or Rangers have been crowned champion in 110 of the 135 top-flight seasons, with it being 40 years since their last unsuccessful season. The Glasgow based clubs compete in the Old Firm derby, known as one of the most emotionally charged fixtures in European football.

In the 2011/12 season, Rangers FC experienced financial difficulties that eventually led to their suspension and demotion into the fourth tier of Scottish football for the 2012/13 season. They returned to the topflight for the 2016/17 season after three promotions in four years. Their absence from the league for four seasons offers an insight into the commercial impact their relegation had on Celtic, a club losing not only their biggest rival, but also the only competitor that has ever been able to challenge them, leaving the league with seasons devoid of any season-long uncertainty.

This systematic literature review will begin by discussing the methodology for how the question was researched before moving into the main literature review section. This review is grounded in the theoretical framework of competition and competitive balance developed by Neale in 1964 and uses this framework to expand into the impact on commercial performance. The literature review will be broken into multiple sections, first reviewing literature surrounding the impact of a structurally dominant team lacking meaningful competition and a rival in a league. Of the impacts discussed, a lack of league quality causing uncertainty of outcome and fan disengagement were identified as factors which had the greatest impact on the structurally dominant team, so they will be reviewed in greater depth. Using the literature researched, the impact that these factors have on the commercial success of the club will then be examined. The review will then move to evaluating the implications of the research. It will do this by exploring the commercial impact of Rangers FC’s absence from the Scottish Premier League on Celtic FC using secondary sources such as financial documents and news articles from the time. This research will be compared to the narrative exposed by the literature to see whether the conceptual impact of a team lacking a competitive rival is mirrored by a real-life example.

This review will be focused on European football and hence referring to European competitions.

Methodology

Inclusion Criterion

Only studies that offered insight into the impacts of an absent competitive rival were included. Incorporated were studies from many research angles such as sports economics, fan psychology, business management and competitive balance. Only literature written in English was included.

Literature identification

Due to the multifaceted nature of the question, the search for literature began by identifying the stand-out themes that would be discussed. The topics originally chosen were competitive balance, rivalry dynamics and psychology, fan behaviour and consumer trends, though it was expected that these topics would expand as the research continued. Keyword searches of “competitive balance in football”, “rivalry in sport” and “fan identity in sport” were conducted. Based upon the title, any relevant sources were downloaded to be evaluated further.

Scopus and Business Source Complete were used to research for sources. To keep the literature researched up-to-date and relevant, the publication date was limited to 2005 onwards, though in cases of backward searching, reoccurring, important sources written prior to 2005 that underpinned much of the literature written by scholars were also included. On Scopus and Business Source Complete, string searches were conducted to deviating success. On Scopus, a broad search of ‘Competitiveness AND sport AND business’ limited to the subject areas of ‘Business’, ‘Economics’ and ‘Psychology’ returned 53 results, which after vetting found none of them suitable for further evaluation. A more focused search on Scopus of “institutional performance" OR "club performance" OR "organisational success" OR "institutional impact" AND "professional sports teams" OR "football clubs" OR "sports organisations" AND "absence" OR "lack" OR "removal" AND "competitive balance" OR "league competition" OR "sporting competition" returned zero results, so research continued using Business Source Complete. The string search ‘football’ or ‘Soccer’ AND ‘rivalry’ OR ‘Competition’ or ‘Competitor’ AND ‘Impact’ limited to the subject areas ‘Sports Competitions’, ‘Incentive (Psychology)’ and ‘Soccer’ returned 312 results. After reviewing the titles of these articles, fourteen were identified as relevant. In these articles, backward searching was used which was the source of thirty-five potential studies. Three additional studies of Scottish culture and football were identified through library searches. Overall, fifty-two potential articles were obtained.

Screening for Inclusion

The abstract of all fifty-two articles were read to see if they were relevant to the impact of an absence of competitive rivalry on a structurally dominant football club’s commercial performance. Eleven sources were discarded, leaving forty-one studies that were significant and downloaded to be read in full.

Eligibility assessment

A detailed plan for the structure of the systematic literature review was created to allow each article to be attributed to a section of the review. This gave the opportunity to easily assign articles to their relevant section and find a narrative within the literature to base the review on. In doing this process, forty-one articles were read and checked for the quality of their publishing journal. In total nine articles were excluded; all were disregarded due to a lack of relevance to the topic.

Literature Review

Introduction

In sport, competition is fundamental to the structure and commercial success of leagues. One of the earliest academics to capture this idea was Neale (1964) who argued that unlike most industries where monopolies are advantageous, a monopoly for a professional sports team would be disastrous. This is because the product of sport itself is competition and interest and value cannot be generated without opponents, so competition is necessary to maximise profits. Neale’s analysis introduced the idea that the league in which teams play in is a natural monopoly as the league can satisfy the markets’ demand at a lower cost than if two or more firms supplied the product. This framework remains true today. For example, the men’s English football pyramid functions as a natural monopoly for Men’s, English 11-a-side football, while other variants (e.g., 5-a-side, women’s leagues), serve different markets and are not direct substitutes. While Neale’s analysis was very insightful for its time, the approach is rooted in theoretical economics, and the subsequent globalisation of sports and evolution of leagues has created layers of complexity that challenge the assumption that the presence of competition alone ensures market viability. In a scenario where formal competition exists, i.e., a team has opponents and fixtures, but quality, uncertainty of outcome and sporting jeopardy are lacking, questions arise about the organisation’s performance. Specifically, how does the absence of competitive rivalry affect the wider commercial performance of a structurally dominant team, which is the research question for this literature review.

The benefits of being a structurally dominant club

Persistent dominance by a single football club within a domestic league presents a multifaceted challenge, impacting stakeholders across the footballing ecosystem. Goossens (2006) explains that competitive imbalance can undermine the credibility and appeal of a league to neutral and global audiences, where the absence of uncertainty diminishes entertainment value and narrative intrigue. This study will focus on the apparent beneficiary of such an environment, the structurally dominant team. In the domestic league structure ‘chance’ is as limited as it possibly can be to ensure the best team wins (Beck, et al, 2025). This allows consistent success for a structurally dominant team which guarantees access to greater domestic prize funds, qualification for UEFA competitions, and enhanced brand equity, all of which consolidate commercial advantage and attract global fanbases. This can create a self-perpetuating imbalance where structurally dominant clubs are able to adopt a win-maximisation strategy, continually reinvesting the substantial prize money accrued season after season to the point where leagues become two tier systems split into predetermined categories of winners and losers (Basini et al, 2023).

A win-maximisation approach has been shown to drastically improve a club’s expected points over a season compared to profit-maximisation, a model that limits sporting quality and erodes ability to compete. Nonetheless it is a model that financially constrained, less successful clubs are often compelled to take to ensure financial stability (Garcia-del-Barrio and Szymanski, 2009). Arrondel, et al (2025) present a nuanced critique of the profit-maximising model for European football. They hypothesise that clubs that solely maximise profit may not financially outperform, and in some cases underperform, those that adopt hybrid win-profit-maximising objectives. Their findings imply that a strict profit-maximising approach can cause suboptimal long-term financial outcomes as it will undermine sporting success and therefore fan engagement and revenue generation. In a league with a structurally dominant club however, a hybrid approach taken by competitors will fall short sporting-wise, as the dominant club can simultaneously pursue win-maximisation while remaining profitable due to commercial exposure and prize money. As a result, the sporting gap between them and any competition remains large and may even widen over time. Empirical studies have shown that the disparity in football clubs’ revenues within a league is strongly dependent on sporting achievements and that there is huge financial inequality between the winners and the losers (Aguiar-Noury and Garcia-del-Barrio, 2022).

Structurally dominant clubs benefit from easier recruitment of both domestic and international players, as consistent participation in European competitions make them attractive destinations for talent. In Scotland, Celtic and Rangers have historically been able to recruit from abroad, whereas clubs outside the ‘Old Firm’ have relied more heavily on developing homegrown Scottish talent (Giulianotti, 2005). However, this poses two key drawbacks, firstly that, promising domestic talent is often eventually acquired by Celtic and Rangers, who’s financial and sporting advantage allow them to attract the best domestic players. Secondly, even if non-Old Firm clubs could retain Scotland’s best players, the national talent pool alone is not competitive internationally, with the men’s team currently ranked 47th globally. Therefore, the Old Firm become stronger and consolidate their place as the best in the league for years to come. This shows how the absence of competitive rivalry can positively affect the wider commercial performance of a structurally dominant football club.

The negative consequences of being a structurally dominant club

And yet academic literature suggests that this causes domestic and international commercial hardship for the structurally dominant team. There are many challenges that arise for them in a league due to the absence of a competitive rival. Kilduff, et al. (2010) adopted the idea that rivalries between firms are extremely positive for motivation, performance and innovation, as rivals ‘strive for increased performance even in times of prosperity’. On top of this, Kilduff, et al (2010) state that when rivalry is lacking, managerial complacency and competitive inertia occur, leading to reduced performance. In a league system, while every match has the same three points on the line, in matches against rivals, motivation is higher, and greater effort is displayed in the performance compared to a matchup against a non-rival (Kilduff, 2014). In the case of Celtic and Rangers, the rivalry stems from centuries of political and religious unrest in the form of British, Protestant Royalists (Rangers) vs. British-colonised, Catholic, Irish settlers (Celtic). In Scotland, the Protestant and Catholic rivalry dates to the 1600’s, and to this day the Old Firm derby embodies the tensions from all those years ago causing it to be one of the most charged matches in European football (Bradley, 2022).

These challenges exemplify the broader consequences observed in football when such a rivalry is absent. Structurally dominant clubs may experience a decline in performance intensity, consequently causing reduced incentive to invest in high-quality talent as the sporting returns on such investment diminish without meaningful opposition. This complacency could have severe consequences in European competitions where the calibre of opponents is much greater than anything encountered domestically, especially for a Scottish club.

Kilduff et al. (2010) also highlight what is arguably the most critical financial consequence of the absence of rivalry, its impact on fan engagement. They argue that fan attendance is directly linked to rivalry, noting that match attendance and overall spectator motivation are significantly influenced by the presence of intense competition. Furthermore, the existence of a structurally dominant team will cause certainty of outcome. Özaydin and Donduran (2019) argue that a team that earns greater revenue than its competitors year on year will eventually dominate them all, becoming a structurally dominant team. This domination decreases uncertainty of outcome at a match, and season-long level, due to the relative quality of the competition. There is a mixed consensus in literature that uncertainty of outcome is linked to fan engagement in sport. Some empirical studies of football attendances have shown that uncertainty of outcome is positively correlated to fan attendances (Forrest and Simmons, 2002; Borland and MacDonald, 2003).

Borland and MacDonald (2003) argue that the demand for sport is fundamentally driven by fan interest, which itself is shaped by both identification with a team and the unpredictability of the outcome of the match. However, this relationship is not universally observed in all contexts. Buraimo (2008) and Buraimo and Simmons (2015), in studies on the English Premier League, found that uncertainty of outcome does not have a significant impact on attendance or TV viewership. This divergence may be attributed to the unique characteristics of the Premier League, which has an in-elastic demand due to its global popularity, which garners consistently high attendance and TV viewership. However, these studies did find that the quality of the teams involved in a match increased demand, and that an uncertain-outcome, high-quality match would have greater viewership than an uncertain-outcome, low-quality match. In the context of Celtic and Rangers, these are the clubs of the greatest quality in the Scottish pyramid so theoretically the more uncertain the match and league is, the greater viewership will be. A study that looked at the Scottish league found that uncertainty of outcome does positively impact viewership as well (Allan and Roy, 2008).

The commercial performance of a structurally dominant football club will be negatively impacted by the absence of a competitive rival as the club loses an emotional intensity that drives its fan engagement and the players performance, as well being without a high-quality opponent to create engagement and uncertainty of outcome in the league.

The impact of an absence of competitive rivalry on fan engagement and emotional investment in sport

Goossens (2006) discusses the impact competitive rivalry has for the neutral sports fan. There is a greater appreciation for a game when it is balanced and uncertain, using the phrase ‘you never know in sports, anything can happen’ to articulate the necessity for excitement and balance. In a situation where the outcome of a game feels predetermined, there is no excitement for a neutral fan, creating disengagement. Garcia-del-Barrio and Reade (2022) used Formula One to study how certainty of outcome impacted fan and general public engagement in the sport. Where their study differs from many is that it examines the uncertainty of the entire Formula One championship as well as the uncertainty of a single race. Using Google Trends data, they were able to show that as the Formula One season’s winner became more predictable, interest in the ongoing season decreases greatly. Any interest that does remain is in the individual races. Whilst this study was not based on football, its findings are applicable to other sports with the authors extending their study as valuable comprehensions for all sports leagues to consider.

There is a similar impact on a fan that is supporting the structurally dominant team. Aiken, et al (2021) studied overdominance in basketball, highlighting that success can paradoxically undermine fan engagement. Their findings indicate that certainty of outcome was decreasing attendance and reducing ticket sales. Crucially, the study also observed a change in fan psychology. As winning became routine, the emotional satisfaction fans achieved when their team won was blunted as it had become too normalised. Fans began to view success not as an achievement, but an expectation, decreasing their devotion to the team.

Berendt and Uhrich (2016), argue that rivalry helps fans define themselves with who they are and a lack of such will reduce ‘public collective self-esteem, perceived distinctiveness and ingroup cohesion’ which explains a fans’ underlying need and desire for rivalry. In an exert interviewing German football fans, a supporter of Borussia Dortmund expressed that the relegation of the clubs’ biggest rival, Schalke 04, would reduce the enjoyment of being a sports customer (Uhrich, 2014). Uhrich describes this as intensifying, where relationships between supporters of different clubs develops into rivalries or friendships. This is usually seen for clubs in geographical proximity to each other, with the relationship between them developing over a long period of time, perhaps the best example of such being the Old Firm. This relationship between supporters of rival clubs’ breeds engagement, through means such as friendly banter, discussions, excitement and great celebration should a team be victories in a derby match. Uhrich even claims that there is as much reason for fans to celebrate when their rival team loses as there is when their own team is victorious. A similar sensation is observed by Havard (2014), where ‘Glory Out of Reflected Failure’ (GORFing) is used to explain the euphoria sports fans feel when their favourite teams rival is defeated. The practise of intensifying and GORFing create additional value for club and fan alike, and in the absence of a rivalry, that value is lost.

The impact on the wider commercial performance

Matchday Revenue

It is clear in literature that a lack of rivalry and competitive balance in a league has many detrimental effects, but how do these theoretical impacts translate into negative commercial performance for a structurally dominant club in such a league? Fan disengagement is a multifaceted issue that causes problems in many areas of commercial performance for a football club. Many of these issues boil down to the fact that rivalry and meaningful competition create value and without it, that value is lost for stakeholders. Intense rivalries are considered a desirable aspect of sport consumption, and without it fans are less inclined to attend a match (Uhrich, 2014; Pawlowski and Anders, 2012). A significant component of the in-person match experience is the heightened atmosphere created by the presence of intense rivalries, often manifested through charged interactions between opposing supporters. This atmosphere contributes to a sense of occasion and unpredictability, enhancing the value of the event. In the absence of a meaningful rivalry however, this emotional intensity is diminished, resulting in less vibrant and socially engaging matchdays. Also, the decreased quality and uncertainty of outcome from less competition reduces attendance and spectator interest, as matches become increasingly predictable and less engaging (Kesenne, 2006). For the structurally dominant club, this decline in attendance translates directly into reduced matchday revenues, not only through lower ticket sales but also through decreased secondary spending on merchandise and refreshments (Allan and Roy, 2008). On top of this, rivalry games also spike attendance and viewership, so the lack thereof during the season is a loss of greater potential revenue that the club is unable to achieve.

Revenue Sharing

A structurally dominant team may face drawbacks arising from a necessity to sustain competitive balance within the league. Özaydin and Donduran (2019) suggest that dominance in a league only holds meaning based on the opposition faced. To preserve long-term fan engagement and maintain the league’s overall appeal, the dominant club may be compelled to support policies such as revenue sharing, at the expense of its own immediate financial interests. By redistributing resources to bolster weaker clubs, the dominant club helps to ensure the relevance and competitiveness of its own league. Andrews and Harrington (2016) even argue that clubs other than the elite in the world are not financially strong enough to compete and survive at the same time and therefore revenue sharing must be used to retain competition, especially in leagues outside of the ‘big 5’ (England, Spain, Italy, France and Germany). In the long term this may be detrimental to the sporting success of the dominant club, but beneficial to the league, as they have forfeited a portion of their earned revenue to ensure the success of their primary competition.

Broadcasting Revenue

The structurally dominant club may face significant long-term commercial disadvantages stemming from devalued broadcasting agreements. Media rights have become the most substantial sources of revenue for the majority of professional clubs (Bergantiños and Moreno-Ternero, 2020). However, the rules for which leagues distribute this income varies considerably, depending on the regulatory frameworks in place. Crucially, the value of these broadcasting deals is not intrinsic; it is dependent on the perceived quality and competitiveness of the league’s product (Rocaboy, 2023). A decline in competitive balance undermines audience interest, thereby reducing viewership and in turn diminishing the willingness of broadcasters to pay premium fees (Forrest and Simmons, 2002; Allan and Roy, 2008; Borland and MacDonald, 2003).

Furthermore, rivalry matches are the most commercially valuable fixtures in the football calendar. The absence of a rivalry reduces the league’s marketability, when compared to a league with major rivalries, both domestically and globally (Kilduff et al, 2010). This lack of narrative tension and audience engagement weakens the league’s bargaining power in media negotiations, ultimately resulting in a smaller total broadcasting revenue pool to distribute amongst clubs. The implications of this decline are twofold: not only does the structurally dominant club receive a smaller share of a reduced revenue pool, but the weakened financial position of the rest of the league further exacerbates the competitive imbalance.

The relative undesirability of a league where competitive balance is flawed and rivalry is absent as a broadcast product is further complicated by the growing availability of substitutes in the global football market. Wallrafen et al. (2018) provide compelling evidence that, even given the emotional commitment of sports fans, substitution exists when watching football. Their study of fourth tier German Football revealed that attendance at matches declined by 19% when scheduled alongside UEFA Champions League fixtures, and by 23% when coinciding with Bundesliga matches. Although the study focuses on live attendance, their findings imply that fans gravitate towards higher-quality or more prestigious football even when domestic alternatives exist. This behaviour likely extends to broadcast audiences as well, indicating that leagues lacking marquee fixtures may struggle to maintain viewership against one that does and therefore earn a lower media rights valuation.

Sponsorship revenue

Sponsorship revenue is very sensitive to commercial visibility and emotional resonance in football, both of which are undermined by a lack of competitiveness and the absence of a major rivalry, which as discussed, reduces fan engagement and TV and in-person attendance at games. Reduced attendance directly limits the number of spectators who are exposed to sponsorship assets such as pitch-side advertising boards or merchandise. Therefore, the return on investment for sponsors lessens as they rely on fans building brand familiarity and awareness through repeated visual exposure to increase sales (Biscaia et al, 2014). Lower fan engagement also correlates with decreased matchday merchandise sales, including replica kits which are sponsored numerous times, with fans effectively becoming walking advertisements when wearing them. At the same time, reduced television viewership reduces the reach of televised brand placement, such as post-match interview backdrops. These exposure points are critical for a sponsor’s return on investment and a lack of sufficient brand coverage will fail to increase brand recognition and purchase intent (Konstantoulaki et al, 2017).

For the structurally dominant club, the absence of a fixture with a rival leaves sponsors without one of the most valuable sponsorship activations available. Due to their cultural significance and larger audience, these high-profile matches provide heightened emotional involvement from fans which amplifies the effectiveness of the sponsor’s message (Berendt and Uhrich, 2016). Furthermore, it has been shown that the ‘rivalry reference effect’, where a brand references or mocks its competitor in their public communications, is a successful means of advertisement for brands (Berendt et al, 2024). Without a rival club to reference in advertisements, brands lose out on potential value that could be earned had they sponsored a different club.

In a study held on Formula One, Jensen and Cobbs (2014) found that the return on investment from a sponsorship was either negative or extremely low. Their suggestions stated that a brand should only consider sponsorship of a Formula One car if they could ‘seek maximum leverage of multiple sponsorship-linked marketing activities’, something a brand would be unable to do should they sponsor a football club that lacks a competitive rival.

Overall, the financial impact of a lack of competitive rivalry on a structurally dominant club is mixed. The club benefits from being the wealthiest and most modernly successful club in the league, meaning prize money and global attention are, to a degree, guaranteed. However, given the evidence in the literature discussed, it is plausible and expected that the negative effects outweigh the positives and the commercial performance of a club worsens when the league they partake in lacks a competitive rival to challenge them. This phenomenon will be applied and studied in the case of Celtic FC who in 2012, lost their greatest rival and the only team that has ever been able to challenge them in Scottish football over the past 40 years, Rangers FC.

Implications

In 2012, The Rangers Football Club plc entered administration due to significant debts that it owed to HM Revenue and Customs. In April 2012 it was reported that Rangers’ estimated debts were over £134 million and in June, Rangers was liquidated (BBC News, 2012). It was proposed that the club would re-enter the top division of Scottish football for the 2012/13 season, but after a vote by SPL clubs, Rangers were denied by a 10-1 majority, with Celtic rejecting the proposal and the only vote in favour of re-entry being from Rangers themselves. A new company, The Rangers Football Club Ltd, was formed and Rangers were entered into the Third Division (fourth tier) of Scottish Football for the 2012/13 season. Going into the season, Celtic were reigning champions with a league wide expectation of title retention in the first topflight Scottish League season without Rangers ever. To best analyse the impact of Rangers’ absence on Celtic’s commercial performance, financial data, news articles and sporting statistics spanning eight years, two before, four during and two after Rangers’ absence have been collected. Using the narratives exposed in theoretical literature, evidence from this real-life example will be used to showcase how the wider commercial performance of a structurally dominant club is impacted by the absence of competitive rivalry. This will begin by first looking at areas in which Celtic benefitted from Rangers’ absence before moving into the negative areas. Using Celtic’s yearly financial statements dating back to 2011 and news articles, the commercial impact that Rangers’ absence had on Celtic will be analysed and exposed. Footballing statistics such as attendances or squad details have been acquired using TransferMarkt (2025) and financial data is from publicly published companies houses.

Positive consequences of Rangers’ absence

During the four years Rangers were absent from the Scottish Premier League, Celtic consolidated their position as the league’s structurally dominant club, securing the title comfortably in each campaign. Across this period, they lost only 16 league games in total while winning the league by 15 points or more every season, including by an astounding 29-point margin in 2013/14.

This structural dominance translated into potential financial advantages, particularly through access to UEFA competitions. As winners of the league, Celtic were entered into Champions League qualifications which if unsuccessful in, leads to joining the next round of Europa League qualification dependent on which round of Champions League qualifying they lost in. This is financially the greatest benefit of Rangers absence, as qualification to the Champions League guarantees hugely supplemented income through broadcasting rights, ticket sales and prize money. Celtic capitalised in the first two seasons without Rangers by successfully qualifying for the Champions League, leading to a 311% and 253% increase respectively in broadcasting revenue when compared to 2011/12. These earnings were supplemented further by greater ticket sales and prize money. However, in the 2014/15 and 15/16 campaigns they were unable to take advantage of the lack of competition, qualifying only for the Europa League, drastically reducing their revenue, showing the limitations of relying on Champions League qualification to guarantee revenue growth.

The domestic prize fund earned for winning the league four times will have been roughly ten million pounds in total, which in the grand scheme of Celtic’s finances, is inconsiderable over a four-year period of £250 million in earned revenue (Spence, 2013). Celtic were able to successfully employ a win-maximisation approach during Rangers absence that no team in Scottish football could compete with, widening the disparity dramatically. In the 2014/15 season, runners-up Aberdeen achieved a record turnover of £13 million, Celtic’s revenue was nearly four times higher at £50 million, despite it being their lowest-earning campaign of the eight-year period analysed (Aberdeen Football Club Limited, 2015). This gap in financial resources created a two-tier system within Scottish football, where Celtic operated on a different scale to the rest of the league.

Squad composition further illustrates this imbalance. Of the 33 players to feature for Celtic in the 14/15 season, only 12 were Scottish, while Aberdeen fielded 19 Scottish and 10 non-Scots, only 1 of which was from outside the British Isles (TransferMarkt, 2025). This demonstrates how Celtic’s consistent participation in European competitions and higher financial capacity allowed them to recruit international talent while their domestic competition remained reliant on developing and retaining homegrown players.

Celtic’s ability to consolidate itself as the structurally dominant club in Scottish football translated into league success that has lasted for years. Since Rangers’ relegation in 2012, Celtic have won every Scottish Premier League bar one, making it 12 titles in 13 seasons.

The negative impacts of Rangers’ absence

The absence of Rangers as a direct competitor appeared to influence the mentality and motivation within the Celtic squad, contributing to a degree of competitive inertia in domestic fixtures. As the intensity of the league’s competition diminished, Celtic secured the league title in the first season without Rangers with the lowest points tally recorded by a champion in over a decade. Furthermore, in an interview with Glasgow based newspaper, Daily Record, former Celtic players from that period have since reflected that the lack of a competitive rival allowed the team to divert greater focus towards the Champions League, believing that the domestic title could be secured with relative ease (McDonald, 2025). This shift in priority suggests that, without the heightened psychological impact provided by rivalry-driven fixtures, overall performance in the domestic league may have been affected despite Celtic’s continued success.

Rangers’ absence in the league had a significant impact on Celtic’s fan engagement, with stadium attendance patterns providing a clear indicator of shifting supporter behaviour. The removal of season-long uncertainty of outcome surrounding the title race weakened the emotional intensity associated with league fixtures, leading to a gradual decline in league match average attendance at Celtic Park. In 2011/12, prior to Rangers’ relegation, Celtic’s average league attendance was 50,904 per league game, equating to 84% of Celtic Park’s maximum capacity. In the first season without Rangers, that number dropped to 77%, but the financial effect was offset by participation in the Champions League that season, which caused a 10% increase in matchday revenue.

However, as the seasons progressed, Celtic’s average attendance continued to decrease, and without Champions League football in 14/15 and 15/16, so did their matchday revenue. Average home attendances at Celtic Park fell below 45,000, with the stadium operating at approximately 74% capacity, contributing to reduced matchday revenue. The contrast following Rangers’ return to the league highlights the importance of rivalry to sustaining long-term supporter interest. Celtic’s attendance rose sharply in 16/17 as they averaged 55,667 (92% capacity) rising to 56,060 the following season, alongside record matchday revenues. These patterns suggest that the Old Firm rivalry is a critical factor for fan engagement and stadium demand for games involving and absent of Rangers. As the years have passed, Celtic’s attendances have continued to rise to a consistent 96% attendance every year in the league. The resurgence in engagement following Rangers’ reintegration into the league demonstrates that competitive tension and rivalry fixtures are essential components of Celtic’s matchday experience and fan engagement.

Rangers’ absence had an adverse effect on the other teams in the league which eroded their ability to be competitive, which further reduced the quality and uncertainty of outcome of the league season. The clubs in the Scottish Premier League are heavily dependent on matchday revenue earned year on year and could guarantee two sell out games every season against Celtic and Rangers. It was calculated that every team, other than Celtic, would lose roughly £1 million per season in ticket revenue without the fixtures against Rangers. Of the 11 teams except Celtic participating in the 2012/13 SPL season, all of them earned turnover of under £8 million (Goodley, 2012). Losing out on roughly 12.5% of their revenue for 4 seasons in a row significantly hinders every other team in the Scottish Premier League and reduces their ability to compete. To financially survive the clubs may have used a profit-maximising approach rather than a hybrid win-profit-maximisation strategy which ultimately decreased the competitiveness of the league even further.

The impact of Rangers’ absence on Celtic’s financial success

Matchday Revenue and Attendance Driven Income

Declining fan engagement during Rangers’ absence had a significant effect on Celtic’s matchday revenue, which had historically been their most reliable income source. Prior to Rangers’ demotion, Celtic earned £31.1 million in 2010/11 and £29.1 million in 2011/12 despite limited participation in European competitions. By comparison, during 2012/13 and 13/14, when Celtic qualified for the Champions League, matchday revenue rose only slightly to £32.8 million and £28.4 million respectively. Given the significant revenue uplift normally associated with Champions League qualification, driven by increased demand for tickets, this relatively small improvement highlights the financial void left by the absence of the Old Firm.

The effect becomes more apparent in 2014/15 and 15/16, when Celtic competed in the Europa League. Across these two seasons, the club generated a combined £52.7 million in matchday revenue, a £9 million reduction compared with the two seasons prior to Rangers’ relegation. The return of Rangers in 16/17 and 17/18 reversed this trend dramatically. Celtic earned £80.7 million over these two seasons, representing a 53% increase compared to the two preceding seasons. Importantly, these figures coincide with Celtic’s renewed participation in the Champions League, yet the increase in matchday revenue of £19 million relative to the two previous Champions League seasons illustrates that Rangers’ return significantly amplified demand beyond what European competition could generate alone.

Broadcasting Revenue

The Scottish Premier League’s broadcasting deal is a clear indicator of the value that Celtic and Rangers as a collective bring to Scottish Football. For the upcoming 2012/13 season, Sky signed a 5-year, £80 million deal, for the rights to the Scottish Premier League. This represented an increase of £3 million per year from the previous deal signed in 2009. It was noted that ‘central to the deal is the continuation of four Old Firm matches each season’ (Murray, 2011). The agreement was to screen 30 Scottish Premier League games per season, running until the end of the 16/17 season. During the period where Rangers’ demotion was merely a rumour, a source at Sky was quoted as saying “If Rangers are not in the SPL, that would change things for us, naturally. It would leave a quality hole in Scotland’s top division. Competition would deteriorate and, in that event, we would have to renegotiate.” (SportBusiness, 2012). Days before the season started when Rangers were officially demoted, Sky changed the deal. This reduced the agreement by 37.5% to £50 million over 5 years, a £15 million decrease on the deal signed in 2009. On top of this, it was changed from being solely Scottish Premier League coverage as Sky were to broadcast 5 Rangers matches per season as part of the 30-game agreement (Goodley, 2012). This offers a distinct monetary value of the Old Firm for Sky, it was to make up only 13.3% of the broadcasted games every season (4/30) and yet was worth 37.5% (£30m/80m) of the entire broadcasting deal.

Celtic’s broadcasting revenue represented a modest component of their overall income prior to Rangers’ absence, amounting to £7.2 million in 2010/11 and £9.1 million in 2011/12. This dynamic shifted significantly during the first two seasons without Rangers, as Champions League qualification enabled Celtic to generate £51 million in total broadcasting revenue across the two campaigns. However, failure to secure Champions League football in 2013/14 and 14/15 saw revenues fall sharply to £25.7 million over the same time period, showing the fragility of this income stream. The scale of the impact of Rangers’ absence becomes most apparent following their return. Celtic’s broadcasting revenue surged to £77 million, exceeding their previous Champions League seasons by £26 million. This trajectory demonstrates that Rangers’ absence severely constrained Celtic’s broadcasting revenue, illustrating the role of rivalry and competition in sustaining effective commercial performance.

Sponsorship and total revenue

Celtic’s sponsorship revenue was the least impacted revenue stream as a result of Rangers’ demotion. While Celtic and Rangers’ joint Tennent’s lager front of shirt sponsor was ended in 2013, Celtic did not face obvious revenue hardship. They averaged £13.2 million in revenue across the seasons Rangers were absent from the league. This was a slight decrease from the £13.8 million they averaged before they were relegated and a more exaggerated lower figure when compared to the £17.1 million they averaged after Rangers rejoined the league. It is clear greater sponsorship, and merchandising opportunities came around when Rangers re-entered the league, however the fluctuations are not as drastic as Celtic’s other revenue sources.

An analysis of Celtic’s total revenue over this period highlights the significant financial consequences of Rangers’ absence from the Scottish Premier league. While participation in the Champions League boosted overall income, these gains masked the deep vulnerability in Celtic’s financial model. During the seasons when Rangers were absent, Celtic’s revenue was heavily dependent on Champions League qualification and in years where the club failed to qualify, both revenue and profitability declined. The true extent of Rangers’ influence is most apparent following their re-entry to the league. In 2016/17, Celtic’s total revenue surged by £38 million compared to the previous season, followed by an additional £11 million increase in 2017/18. Although these seasons coincided with Champions League participation, the uplift cannot be solely attributed to revenue generated by partaking in the competition. Celtic earned £15 million and £26 million more in 16/17 and 17/18 respectively, than their highest-earning season during Rangers’ absence, despite also playing in the Champions league that year.

Since Rangers’ return, Celtic’s financial trajectory has remained strong and stable. Total revenue has exceeded £70 million in every season except the COVID-affected campaign, contrasting with the period of Rangers’ absence where revenue fell below £70 million in three of the four seasons. This divergence shows the importance of the Old Firm rivalry, not only to Celtic’s sporting competitiveness but also to their commercial performance.

Recommendations

The literature reviewed and implications of Rangers’ absence demonstrate that the absence of the Old Firm derby and lack of competitive balance within the Scottish League was commercially detrimental, not only for Celtic but to the league as a whole. To mitigate such risks and create long-term sustainability, it is recommended that the Scottish League adopt revenue sharing mechanisms, with particular emphasis on distributing income generated from UEFA competitions. As highlighted in the literature, higher-quality opposition stimulates greater fan engagement, leading to higher matchday attendances, broadcasting appeal, and sponsorship revenues for Celtic. Presently, access to significant revenue from UEFA competitions remains a privilege that exclusively benefits Celtic and Rangers, limiting the ability of the other clubs in the league to compete. A revenue-sharing model would serve as a means to redistribute a portion of the resources earned to increase the competitive balance of Scottish football to reduce the inequality in the league, which would be beneficial for Celtic, the Scottish leagues and the national team.

Conclusion

This systematic literature review set out to evaluate how the absence of a competitive rivalry affects the wider commercial performance of a structurally dominant football club, using Celtic FC during Rangers FC’s relegation from the Scottish Premier League as a case study. By synthesising theoretical perspectives, empirical research and eight years of financial and commercial data, this review has demonstrated that the absence of meaningful competition and rivalry present both short-term and long-term challenges to dominant clubs operating within imbalanced leagues.

This review began by discussing the methodology for how academic literature was gathered, analysed, researched and included in this project. By using string searches across multiple databases, a set of sources was established which allowed the use backwards searches to find further suitable sources that offered importance to the question. Upon doing so and screening for eligibility, 32 academic sources were found, that this research is based upon.

The literature established the significance of competitive balance, quality and uncertainty of outcome to the commercial sustainability of professional sport. Competition is the core product of football and without quality and uncertainty of outcome, fan engagement diminishes. While reduced competition and rivalry can create temporary advantages for a structurally dominant club through guaranteed domestic success and easier access to European competitions, the findings in the literature reveal significant structural risks. Lower fan engagement, declining broadcast value, reduced sponsorship leverage and competitive inertia are all consequences of a lack of competition and rivalry and are dangerous for the financial health of a football club.

Applying the framework outlined in the literature to Celtic FC revealed a similar dynamic. The case study demonstrated that, although Celtic maintained domestic dominance during Rangers’ absence, this success masked financial underperformance that was made obvious upon Rangers’ return to the league. Champions League qualification provided short-term revenue boosts that concealed reduced matchday, broadcast and sponsorship revenue. Upon Rangers’ return to the top-flight, Celtic’s total revenue had increased by £49 million, matchday revenues were at record highs and attendances had recovered greatly, reflecting the demand created by rivalry and competition.

Overall, this review concludes that the absence of competitive rivalry places structurally dominant clubs in a paradoxical position. While dominance ensures sporting success, it can simultaneously undermine commercial performance by weakening the broader ecosystem that sustains financial growth. For leagues such as the Scottish Premier League, where structural dominance already concentrates resources among a small number of clubs, the loss of competition further deepens inequality and accelerates disengagement among fans. In Celtic’s case, Rangers’ absence exposed the limitations of depending on high-value fixtures to drive revenue. During the time that Rangers were absent from the league, Celtic were financially underperforming greatly and have since flourished having gained back their greatest competitor and biggest rival.

Further research should investigate the commercial impact of a club being structurally dominant in the past. For example, clubs that in recent memory were structurally dominant but have since lost that status, such as Manchester United or Juventus. Research exploring whether their commercial situation has continued to be positive even given years of stagnation and poor domestic performance would be very valuable. Furthermore, research surrounding the impact that this has on fan engagement from younger generations that haven’t experienced the success that made the clubs so big to begin with, should also be explored.
